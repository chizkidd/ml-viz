Igbo Tone-Loss Explorer

Igbo is a tonal language where pitch distinguishes words and careful orthography marks tone with grave and acute accents alongside dot-below vowels (ị, ọ, ụ). This page inspects 21 recordings from one speaker, run through facebook/omniASR-CTC-1B, and compares the model output against the author's unreviewed reference transcripts. Data: measured from omniASR-igbo-blindspots, extracted from the audited evaluation repo. Scores and charts are computed live from those 21 rows.

Read this first. One speaker (Afikpo Igbo), 21 short clips, one model, single-annotator unreviewed references (evidence ceiling: E2 — descriptive counts and alignment conditional on those references). The four task categories mix languages (4 Igbo + 2 Yoruba in tonal; 1 Pidgin in code-switching; 1 French in domain). This is a diagnostic probe, not a prevalence estimate for Igbo ASR or a test of acoustic tone perception.

Beyond counting: are the marks in the right place?

alignment estimate
Count-based scores cannot tell a correct mark from a wrong one: if the reference has an acute accent and the model writes a dot-below letter in the same spot, the count still matches. Why resample clips, not marks, and why more iterations do not help

Drop rate, exploratory 95% bootstrap spread. A bootstrap redraws data with replacement many times and looks at how much the statistic moves. The resampling unit decides the width — and even clip-level intervals are withdrawn in the audited repo because no language group reaches 10 informative clips.

Does character error rate see tone loss?

One dot per clip that has expected marks (n = 18 dependent clips). Switch between Levenshtein normalization profiles (strict, tone_stripped, base_letters, legacy) and the CSV's original difflib dissimilarity score to see how base-letter errors and mark loss separate.

What this can and cannot show

What it supports (up to E2)

  • Conditional on the author's references, the output text on these clips carries far fewer tone marks than the references.
  • On the 4 Igbo tonal clips, none of the 32 reference tone marks is reproduced under base-letter-aware alignment; all 3 output tone marks appear in clip 09 whose reference has none.
  • Separating strict CER (0.416 on the 4 Igbo tonal clips) from tone_stripped (0.197) and base_letters (0.121) shows that transcription errors and mark loss occur together and must be measured separately.

What it does not show

  • That other speakers, dialects or recording conditions behave the same: one speaker, 21 clips, one session/device.
  • That other models behave this way: one model (omnilingual-asr 0.1.0 also ignores the lang argument for CTC models).
  • Why it happens or whether the model perceives acoustic pitch. Clip 09 was spoken with flatter intonation than clip 08 (F0 SD 1.59 vs 2.71 semitones, one production each), not verified monotone, and added dot-below letters there may be valid orthography.
  • That any reference tone mark, word gloss, or minimal-pair contrast is correct: single annotator, no independent fluent-Igbo review (U-1).
  • Clean category comparisons: clips 01–05 use a 16 kHz / 22 kbps export chain with digital-silence gaps while clips 06–21 are 48 kHz / 65 kbps, and Yoruba, French and Pidgin controls sit inside the historical task categories.
