Policy Gradients: REINFORCE & Actor-Critic

Value-based control learns action values and acts nearly deterministically, which breaks when the best policy must genuinely randomize. Policy gradient methods parameterize π(a | s, θ) directly and climb expected return by gradient ascent — first via Monte Carlo REINFORCE, then with variance-reducing baselines, and finally online at every step with Actor-Critic. Everything here is computed live in your browser with fixed seeds.

Short Corridor with Switched Actions (Sutton & Barto, Example 13.1). Four states: s0, s1, s2, and terminal goal G. Every step costs −1 (γ = 1). In s0 and s2, right moves +1 and left moves −1 (clamped at s0). In s1, the actions are switched: right goes back to s0, while left advances to s2. Because all states look identical to the agent, the policy must choose a single probability p = π(right) everywhere.

REINFORCE on the Short Corridor (Sutton & Barto, Figs. 13.1 & 13.2). Both agents start at the poor near-deterministic policy p(right) = 0.05 (expected return −82.1) with identical random streams. Plain REINFORCE multiplies the score ∇ln π by the full return Gt. Adding a learned scalar baseline b = w shifts the update to (Gt − w)∇ln π — zero change to the expected gradient, dramatic drop in variance.

One-Step Actor-Critic vs Monte Carlo REINFORCE (Sutton & Barto, Section 13.5). REINFORCE waits until the episode ends to know Gt. Actor-Critic swaps Gt − v(St) for the online TD error δt = Rt+1 + γ v(St+1) − v(St), updating both critic and actor immediately on every step. Toggle the critic representation on the Short Corridor to see both sides of bootstrapping: huge variance reduction when the critic distinguishes states, versus systematic bias when it cannot.
